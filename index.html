Jiyan Bai

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at the University at Albany, SUNY. I will be on the academic job market during the 2026–2027 academic year.

My research examines how workers’ social comparison behavior interacts with job search decisions, shaping reservation wages, job mobility, wage dispersion, and aggregate labor-market outcomes. I use longitudinal survey data and panel econometric methods to quantify the role of wage rank in job satisfaction. I then develop and calibrate a theoretical on-the-job search model with endogenous search effort and rank-dependent utility to study the implications for unemployment, wage inequality, and social welfare.

Research interests: Macroeconomics, Labor Economics, Search Theory, Wage Dispersion and Inequality, Behavioral Economics.

Research

Job Market Paper

Rank Matters: On-the-Job Search Model with Social Comparison Behavior

Read abstract

This paper studies how workers’ ordinal wage-rank concerns affect individual search behavior and labor-market outcomes. Using the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1997, I show that among similar workers, a 10 percentage point deterioration in wage rank requires approximately a 13 percent wage increase to keep predicted job satisfaction unchanged. I extend an on-the-job search model with endogenous search effort to study the aggregate implications of this comparison behavior. In the model, stronger rank concerns make low-wage jobs less attractive while increasing the option value of search across wages. As a result, rank concerns raise reservation wages and strengthen search incentives. In equilibrium, however, the higher reservation threshold reduces the set of acceptable offers, lowers aggregate job mobility, increases unemployment, compresses accepted-wage dispersion, and reduces social welfare. In a counterfactual exercise where the offer wage dispersion becomes more unequal while the mean offer wage is preserved, rank concerns partially mitigate the adverse mobility effects for low-wage workers by raising the lower bound of accepted wages, while amplifying the welfare gains from a thicker upper tail.
